## What this article promises

Two Forrester reports, read together, describe the same shift from two independent directions. *The AI Platforms Landscape, Q1 2026* [FP, Market Dynamics] is a market report: it counts vendors, sizes revenue, and tracks who is entering a category. *The Architect's Guide To Agentic AI* [FA, Summary] is a practice report: it tells architects how to build one kind of application. Neither is about the other's subject. Yet both land on the same claim, for different reasons — that the center of gravity in enterprise AI has moved off the model and onto the system around it. This article follows that convergence, states plainly where the two reports agree and where they simply don't overlap, and derives one thesis from reading them side by side: the part of the stack now under the most pressure is not the model layer at all. It is the control plane — identity, policy, evaluation, observability — and the two reports disagree, without noticing they disagree, about whether that layer is already solved.

A third report was part of this brief: a Gartner document reached only through a personalized reprint link. That link returned a bot-detection interstitial on every attempt and has no public archive, because reprint links like it are recipient-scoped by design. It is not used here. Where this article needs the *kind* of framing Gartner is publicly known for — vendor evaluation frameworks, governance taxonomies — it says so in general terms and attributes nothing specific to the unread document. The full access log is filed with this article's source notes.

**Evidence labels used throughout:** `[EMPIRICAL]` marks a fact one of the two reports states as researched (a statistic, a named definition). `[PRACTITIONER]` marks an interview quotation reported inside a source. `[SYNTHESIS]` marks this article's own argument — a claim that follows from comparing the sources but that neither source states on its own. `[CONCEPTUAL]` marks a named framework from a source, used once and attributed. Citations point to a specific report section, marked FP for the Platforms Landscape report and FA for the Architect's Guide — for example, a claim traced to the Architect's Guide reads as [FA, Summary].

## Chapter 1. The Model Is No Longer the System

Start with what "AI platform" used to mean. For most of the last decade, an AI platform was, functionally, a model factory: a place to prepare data, train or fine-tune a model, evaluate it against a benchmark, and expose it behind an endpoint. The unit of value was the model. Better model, better product. An application team's job was mostly to call the endpoint and format the output.

Forrester's own market report no longer describes the category this way. It defines an AI platform as software spanning "the AI development lifecycle from data management to inferencing to create composable application components, AI agents, and/or complete applications" [FP, Market Definition]. Read that definition carefully: the *outputs* it names are components, agents, and applications — not models. The model has quietly become an input, one of several "AI technologies including foundation models, predictive models, and agent frameworks" [FP, Market Definition] that a platform assembles into something else.

That is a market-structure claim: Forrester is describing where vendors now compete. The architecture report makes the same relocation for a completely different reason — not because buyers changed what they shop for, but because of how reasoning actually works in an agentic system. Forrester's architects write that "reasoning is built around an FM and its behavior, but it isn't the only thing driving reasoning and planning. Reasoning emerges from the interaction between the model, the execution that manages the agent loop, the structure of context provided to the model, and the constraints imposed during execution" [FA, Reasoning Components]. This is a technical claim, not a marketing one: even holding the model fixed, the same model produces different reasoning quality depending on the loop, the context assembly, and the constraints wrapped around it.

`[SYNTHESIS]` Put the two claims next to each other and something stronger appears than either report says alone. A market report and an architecture report, written by different teams for different audiences, independently stopped treating the model as the unit of value — one because buyers now shop for delivered applications, the other because the technical mechanism of "reasoning" is no longer something a model does by itself. Two unrelated instruments pointing at the same relocation is better evidence than either instrument alone. This is the article's central empirical anchor, and everything that follows is a consequence of taking it seriously.

The practical implication is a distinction worth holding onto for the rest of this article: **model capability is not production AI capability.** A frontier model can ace a benchmark and still produce a bad agent, because the agent's quality is a property of the system — the loop, the context, the tools, the guardrails — not of the model in isolation. Conversely, a mid-tier model wrapped in a well-designed system can outperform a frontier model wrapped in a bad one. Most of this article is about what "the system" is actually made of, and why getting it wrong is now the dominant failure mode.

## Chapter 2. What an AI Platform Actually Has to Do

Before decomposing the agentic layer, it's worth taking Forrester's platform-level picture seriously, because it is more specific than the phrase "AI platform" usually implies. The market report's own architecture diagram [FP, Figure 1, described via caption] lays out two parallel tracks that converge into applications.

One track handles predictive/ML models: data ingestion (batch or streaming, ETL/ELT, change-data-capture) feeds data processing (cleaning, enrichment, vectorization, embeddings), which feeds ML model training (feature stores, hyperparameter tuning), model evaluation (A/B tests, canaries), deployment (GPU management), and ongoing ModelOps (drift and quality, cost and latency, observability). The second track does the same thing for foundation models — training, then "tuning and alignment" (context engineering, fine-tuning, distillation, quantization), then deployment, then its own ModelOps.

Both tracks feed a third layer Forrester calls "AI and agentic applications": planning and process design, retrieval-augmented generation and tool use, UI creation, memory and state, agent testing and evaluation (human-in-the-loop and LLM-as-judge methods), and agentic app monitoring (telemetry and safety, policy gates, risk monitoring, observability). All of it, in Forrester's own picture, sits on top of an "enterprise semantic layer" — ontology, data models, knowledge graphs, lineage, governance, a feature store — and the entire assembly is wrapped in a security, threat, and governance framework [FP, Figure 1].

`[SYNTHESIS]` Two things are worth pulling out of this diagram that the report doesn't say explicitly. First, "ModelOps" appears twice, once per track, but never merges into one discipline in Forrester's own picture — predictive-model operations and foundation-model operations are drawn as separate boxes even though both feed the same downstream applications and both need the same underlying instrumentation (drift, cost, latency, quality). That is an argument, visible only in the diagram's shape, that enterprises still run two operationally distinct model lifecycles side by side, not one unified one, whatever unifying language vendors use in their marketing. Second, notice what is *not* a fourth parallel track: agents. Agentic capability is drawn as something built on top of both model tracks, consuming their outputs, not as a peer lifecycle with its own training/evaluation/deployment loop. That placement is consistent with Chapter 1's claim — the model tracks produce ingredients; the agentic layer is where those ingredients become a system.

A capability model built from this evidence, stated plainly rather than as a vendor checklist, is what an AI platform has to actually provide:

1. **Data movement and preparation** — ingestion, cleaning, vectorization — for both structured/ML and unstructured/generative use.
2. **Model access and lifecycle management** — training or fine-tuning where relevant, evaluation, deployment, and drift/cost/latency operations, for both predictive and foundation models, understood as two disciplines, not one.
3. **Application and agent construction** — planning/process design, retrieval, tool use, UI, memory and state — the layer this article spends the most time on, because it is where FP and FA meet.
4. **A semantic and governance substrate** — the ontology, lineage, and policy layer that both model tracks and the agentic layer are supposed to share, so that "the same customer" or "the same policy" means the same thing everywhere it's checked.
5. **A wrapping security, evaluation, and observability layer** — named by FP as infrastructure, and shown by FA (next chapter onward) to be considerably less mature in practice than its infrastructure billing suggests.

That fifth item is where this article is headed, but first it's worth being honest about what the third report in this brief would have added, and being precise about what the two accessible reports do and don't agree on.

## Chapter 3. Two Reports, Two Lenses

It would be a mistake to read *The AI Platforms Landscape* and *The Architect's Guide To Agentic AI* as if they were competing accounts of the same question. They are not. They are different instruments pointed at adjacent parts of the same stack, and the honest way to compare them is to say what each one is actually built to see.

FP is a **market lens.** It exists to help a buyer choose among 40 named vendors, sized by category revenue ("large" vendors at $250 million or more, "medium" at $100–250 million, "small" at $10–100 million in category revenue [FP, Notable Vendors]), differentiated by geography, industry focus, and deployment model. Its unit of analysis is the vendor. Its central worry is buyer confusion: the report names "widespread confusion among buyers about how to implement and adopt AI" as the market's top challenge [FP, Market Maturity, Figure 2 caption], and its own explicit purpose is to reduce that confusion by mapping vendors to use cases.

FA is an **architecture lens.** It exists to help an architect who has already chosen tools — or is building without a single vendor's opinionated stack — design a working system. Its unit of analysis is the component (runtime, reasoning, memory, tools, guardrails, security, testing, orchestration), not the vendor. Its central worry is not confusion but immaturity: it states plainly that "core engineering and governance practices for agentic AI remain immature despite rapid adoption" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up].

Where they converge is more interesting than either report alone. Both describe the category as still being contested at its boundary. FP: vendors "from markets as diverse as digital process automation (DPA), API management, search and retrieval, and customer service and support are all attempting to build AI platforms" [FP, Market Dynamics] — meaning no single incumbent category owns "AI platform" yet. FA: architects should build "modular, deliberate, and adaptable" systems "rather than a monolithic ideal state" [FA, Summary] — meaning no single canonical architecture exists yet either. A market without a settled category boundary and a discipline without a settled reference architecture are two descriptions of the same immaturity, seen from procurement and from engineering respectively.

Where they diverge is worth stating precisely, because the divergence is the useful part. FP treats governance and observability largely as *available capability* — items on a functionality checklist a platform either has or doesn't, alongside a "semantic layer" and a "security, threat, and governance framework" drawn as an existing wrapper around the stack [FP, Figure 1]. FA treats a specific and central piece of that same governance layer — identity and access management — as "one of the least mature areas," explicitly because "AI agents are a new identity type that introduces novel challenges for governance, authentication, and authorization" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up].

`[SYNTHESIS]` This is not a contradiction in the sense of one report being wrong. FP is describing what vendors are shipping and marketing as a capability; FA is describing whether that capability actually holds up as an engineering discipline once an architect tries to use it for something with real autonomy. Both can be true at once: a governance and semantic layer can exist as a purchasable line item and still be immature as a solved problem. That gap — sold as available, experienced as unfinished — is the throughline for the rest of this article, and it is developed fully in Chapter 6.

## Chapter 4. Anatomy of an Agent

"Agents are AI systems that can autonomously perform tasks" is not an explanation; it is a restatement of the word. Forrester's architecture report does better: it breaks an agentic application into eight component categories — "runtime, reasoning, memory, tool discovery and calling, guardrails and constraints, security and access control, testing and evaluation, and orchestration" [FA, The Eight Components Of Agentic Application Architecture] — and each one carries real engineering weight. Working through them is the fastest way to see why "an agent" is not "a model plus a system prompt."

**Runtime.** An agent calling a foundation model is not one process; Forrester's architects insist runtimes "must be treated as first-class distributed systems, not as lightweight extensions of application logic" [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes]. A single agent typically executes across model endpoints, orchestration services, policy services, tool execution environments, and memory stores, "often span[ning] regions and trust boundaries" [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes]. That has a direct reliability consequence practitioners flagged to Forrester's researchers: production SLAs for agentic systems have to account for *degraded* model behavior, not binary uptime, "because partial capability loss is more common than total failure" [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes]. A model that's slower, or reasoning worse under load, is a normal operating state for an agent runtime — not an outage.

**Reasoning.** Already covered in Chapter 1: reasoning is a property of model, loop, context, and constraints together, with a continuous feedback loop over outcomes required for it to stay accurate [FA, Reasoning Components]. This is the architectural basis for the "model ≠ agent" distinction — an agent built on the same model with a worse loop reasons worse, full stop.

**Memory.** Forrester's architects describe memory as commonly discussed across two to four layers; in their four-layer view: *context memory* is the immediate content loaded into the model that shapes a single output; *session (short-term) memory* is the current conversation and accessed data supporting one continuous workflow; *personal long-term memory* is what a specific user or process has done historically; *institutional long-term memory* is how that user or process connects to the rest of the enterprise, "which enables more precise understanding of how to respond to inputs or execute workflows" [FA, Memory Is An Essential Component]. This is worth pausing on because it makes one distinction the industry constantly collapses: **memory is not the same thing as the context window.** The context window is the mechanism that delivers context-memory content to the model on a given call. Session, personal, and institutional memory are durable stores that decide *what gets loaded into* that window in the first place — and unlike the window, they persist and accumulate across calls, sessions, and even across different agents acting on behalf of the same enterprise process.

**Tool discovery and calling.** Knowing a tool exists is necessary but not sufficient. Forrester's point is specific: "agents execute efficiently only when tool behavior is consistent and unambiguous, enabling intent-level execution without added reasoning overhead" [FA, The Discovery And Proper Use Of Tools]. An inconsistent tool — one whose inputs, error behavior, or side effects vary — forces the agent to spend reasoning budget compensating for the tool instead of using the tool. This is also where **tool access is not the same thing as tool authorization**: an agent can be technically able to call a tool (it's registered, discoverable, schema-valid) while an entirely separate question — should this specific agent, acting for this specific user, in this specific context, be allowed to invoke it right now — remains open. Chapter 6 returns to why that separation matters more than it sounds like it should.

**Guardrails and constraints.** Guardrails are not one mechanism. Forrester names three: traditional business rules (deterministic code), data-science explainability techniques, and foundation-model prompting constraints — operating "across multiple functional levels, from individual agent behavior to organizational policy enforcement" [FA, Guardrails Control Agentic Behavior]. Notably, guardrail decisions are not purely technical: as Capgemini told Forrester's researchers, "there's a cultural element to whether people want an agent to be able to take control" `[PRACTITIONER]` [FA, Guardrails Control Agentic Behavior]. An organization's actual autonomy ceiling is set partly by what its people are willing to trust, not only by what the architecture is capable of enforcing.

**Security and access control.** Agentic systems open new attack surfaces — Atlassian's Brendan Haire named prompt injection directly as a focus area `[PRACTITIONER]` [FA, Agentic Security Requires Responding To New Surface Of Attacks] — but Forrester's larger claim is structural: agentic systems "redefine the trust boundary," because agents "act with intent, with more autonomy, and on behalf of users across systems they can't directly access" [FA, Agentic Security Requires Responding To New Surface Of Attacks]. Traditional identity and access management was built for a human logging in with a session, or a service account with a fixed, narrow scope — not for a delegated actor improvising a plan across systems on a person's behalf. Forrester proposes a response model it calls AEGIS `[CONCEPTUAL]`, built on three stated principles: least agency, high explainability, and adaptable risk assessment and posturing [FA, Agentic Security Requires Responding To New Surface Of Attacks]. This article names AEGIS once, here, as Forrester's framework — not as something this article invented or is extending.

**Testing and evaluation.** Forrester states this plainly as a heading-level claim: testing and evaluation "are inseparable in agentic AI and are an architecturewide concern" [FA, Testing And Evaluation Are Inseparable]. The elaborating detail behind that specific heading did not render in this research pass (disclosed in the source notes), but the claim is corroborated earlier in the same report, where Forrester's researchers note that "testing practices are similarly unsettled... as agentic systems fail in emergent, runtime driven ways that don't resemble traditional software defects" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up]. A conventional software bug is reproducible: same input, same failure. An emergent agentic failure can depend on context assembly, tool latency, and model sampling all lining up a particular way — which is why testing has to live inside the architecture, watching real trajectories, rather than living in a separate pre-release gate.

**Orchestration.** The component that "stitches together the whole package," in Forrester's own heading language [FA, Agentic Orchestration Stitches Together The Whole Package]. It gets its own chapter next, because it is where the other seven components' design choices actually get made concrete.

## Chapter 5. Orchestration Is a Control-Plane Decision, Not a Convenience

It's tempting to treat "add an orchestrator" as an infrastructure upgrade — more capacity, cleaner code, done. Forrester's architecture report is precise evidence against that framing: it describes three architectural patterns, and shows that each one reshapes memory, guardrails, access control, and testability *together*, not as independent knobs.

**Single agent.** The narrowest pattern: one agent, one runtime, used for targeted products and workflows [FA, For Targeted Agentic Products And Workflows]. Simplicity is the whole argument for it.

**Sequential multiagent.** For linear task flows with discrete step boundaries. Work is decomposed into agents that each get their own lightweight runtime and a narrowly scoped reasoning job, "reducing context pressure as compared with a single agent" [FA, For Linear Task Flows]. Memory handoffs between steps are explicit — "transient scratchpads or structured outputs rather than shared long-term memory" — and tool calling becomes more deterministic, because each agent invokes tools aligned to its specific task. Guardrails apply per step, "input validation before handoff, output checks after execution," which shrinks the blast radius of any one failure. Access control gets easier to reason about, because each agent's permissions are narrowly scoped to its task. Teams can unit-test agents independently and trace a linear decision path [FA, For Linear Task Flows]. The cost is rigidity: this pattern "struggle[s] when tasks require dynamic replanning or parallelism" [FA, For Linear Task Flows].

**Multiagent with an orchestrator.** Coordination is externalized to an explicit orchestrator that decomposes, orders, retries, and recovers, while task agents stay atomic. Runtime becomes genuinely distributed: the orchestrator holds execution state while task agents run independently across endpoints and tools. Memory is deliberately split — the orchestrator keeps global state, task agents get only what's relevant to their task. Tool discovery centralizes through registries, so the orchestrator can constrain, at runtime, what any given agent is allowed to invoke. Guardrails shift from prompt-level constraints toward execution-level controls — policy checks, retries, degraded-mode behavior — and Forrester's interviewees were explicit that these controls "are not free and should be factored into SLA costs" [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work]. Access control moves to role-based agent identities rather than direct inheritance from a human user's permissions. In exchange, testing and monitoring mature substantially: an orchestrator makes trajectory analysis, step-level observability, and targeted mid-workflow remediation possible in a way a single agent or a sequential chain cannot support [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work].

`[SYNTHESIS]` Read across the three patterns and a table falls out that Forrester's prose implies but doesn't print as a table:

| Pattern | Memory shape | Guardrail placement | Access-control grain | Testability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single agent | One context, whatever the agent carries | Prompt-level, mostly | Coarse (the agent's own scope) | Whole-trajectory only |
| Sequential multiagent | Explicit handoffs, no shared long-term store | Per-step, input/output checked | Per-task, narrow | High — linear, unit-testable per step |
| Orchestrator-based | Deliberately split: global vs. task-relevant | Execution-level (policy checks, retries, degraded mode) | Role-based per agent identity | Highest — trajectory analysis, step-level observability |

The point of laying it out this way is that **orchestration is not a performance decision. It is a decision about where authority and visibility live.** Choosing an orchestrator pattern because it "scales better" without registering that it also moves guardrails from prompt-level to execution-level, and moves access control from user-inherited to role-based agent identity, means an organization has changed its control plane by accident while thinking it changed its execution plane on purpose. That distinction — between the plane where work actually happens (models, agents, tools, retrieval, actions) and the plane where that work is authorized, watched, and constrained (identity, policy, evaluation, observability) — is the organizing idea for the rest of this article `[SYNTHESIS]`.

## Chapter 6. The Control Plane Problem

Here is the claim this article is built to defend, assembled from evidence in both reports rather than stated by either one on its own: **the control plane is sold as available infrastructure and run as an unfinished discipline, and that gap — not model quality — is where most enterprise agentic programs actually break.**

The "sold as available" half comes from FP. Its own reference architecture wraps the entire stack in "an enterprise semantic layer providing ontology, data models, knowledge graphs, lineage, governance, and a feature store," with "the entire system... wrapped in a security, threat, and governance framework" [FP, Figure 1]. Observability, specifically, is listed as one of Forrester's seven *extended use cases* — something a vendor can specialize in and sell as a product, "as a standalone use case and as a foundational capability of AI applications" [FP, Functionality By Use Case]. In the market's own self-description, governance and observability are not open problems; they're SKUs.

The "run as unfinished" half comes from FA. Identity and access management is explicitly named as "one of the least mature areas" in the entire discipline [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up] — not because vendors haven't built IAM tooling, but because an AI agent is a genuinely new kind of identity, one that acts with delegated intent across systems a human never directly granted it access to [FA, Agentic Security Requires Responding To New Surface Of Attacks]. Testing is "similarly unsettled" for a structural reason: agentic failures are emergent and runtime-driven, unlike the reproducible defects conventional test suites were built to catch [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up]. And the report's empirical anchor makes the cost of that immaturity concrete: 60% of enterprise generative-AI decision-makers named "agentic sprawl" a challenge in Forrester's own Q4 2025 AI Pulse Survey, because "agentic AI is not a single system" — teams independently add agents, tools, prompts, and model endpoints, "multiplying governance and monitoring overhead," while ROI pressure never pauses to let anyone catch up [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up] `[EMPIRICAL]`.

`[SYNTHESIS]` Neither report states the gap this way, but it is the plain consequence of holding both claims at once. A platform vendor's semantic-and-governance layer can be real, useful, and still not address the specific problem Forrester's architects flag as least mature: authenticating and authorizing an autonomous, delegated, cross-system actor in a way that traditional IAM was never designed to do. Buying the governance module does not, by itself, solve the identity problem, because the identity problem is not a missing feature — it's a missing *category*. This is why increasing autonomy makes the control plane matter *more*, not less: a larger action space is a larger failure surface, and a larger failure surface needs more authorization granularity, more observability, more evaluation coverage, and a faster path to human escalation, precisely at the moment an organization is tempted to treat "the platform already has governance" as a checked box.

Two distinctions fall out of this that are worth stating on their own, because they are routinely collapsed in practice:

**Observability is not logging.** A log is a record of what happened at one point. Observability, in the sense FA's testing/monitoring discussion implies, is the ability to reconstruct *why* a multi-step trajectory ended where it did — which context was assembled, which tool was chosen and with what arguments, which guardrail fired or didn't, and where in that chain a degraded model response (Chapter 4's "partial capability loss") first appeared. An orchestrator-based architecture makes this possible through step-level tracing [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work]; a single-agent architecture, by contrast, can usually only be inspected as one opaque trajectory. Logging every call is not the same as being able to answer "why did the agent do that."

**Model evaluation is not system evaluation.** Benchmarking a model answers "is this a good model." It does not answer "does this agent complete this task correctly, safely, and within cost and latency budget, across the tool calls and context assembly it actually goes through in production." FA's insistence that testing is "architecturewide," not a phase gate, is exactly the claim that evaluation has to move from the model to the full trajectory — a claim Chapter 7 develops directly.

## Chapter 7. Evaluation and Observability, From Model to System

The shift from Chapter 1 — value has moved from the model to the system around it — has a direct evaluation consequence, and it is worth making the comparison explicit rather than leaving it implicit.

| Concern | Model-centric view | System-centric view |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of evaluation | A single prompt–response pair | A full execution trajectory: retrieval, reasoning, tool calls, and their consequences |
| Quality signal | Response quality against a benchmark | Task outcome — did the goal actually get accomplished, correctly, and safely |
| Failure mode | A bad generation | A bad execution trajectory — the model reasoned fine but the wrong tool fired, or the context was wrong, or a guardrail didn't trigger |
| Security lens | Prompt-level safety (jailbreaks, harmful content) | Identity, tool permissions, data access, and delegation paths across the whole trajectory [FA, Agentic Security Requires Responding To New Surface Of Attacks] |
| Cost | Cost of one inference call | Cost of an entire multi-step execution, including retries and degraded-mode fallbacks [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes; To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work] |
| Testing method | Static benchmark suite, run pre-release | Trajectory analysis and step-level observability, run continuously [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work] |

`[SYNTHESIS]` This table is not drawn from either report as a table; it is this article's synthesis of claims made separately in both. But every row traces to a specific claim above, and the pattern across rows is consistent: at every level — quality, failure, security, cost, testing — the unit of analysis Forrester's own evidence points to is larger than the model. An organization that keeps its evaluation practice model-centric while its architecture has already gone system-centric is measuring the wrong thing at every layer simultaneously, which is a specific, nameable version of Mistake 5 in Chapter 9.

FP adds one more wrinkle worth keeping. It treats "observability" simultaneously as a foundational capability every AI application needs and as a standalone product category vendors can specialize in and sell [FP, Top Use Cases; Functionality By Use Case]. That is not a contradiction so much as a genuinely awkward market position: a capability that is infrastructure for everyone is also, at the same time, someone's whole product. It means an enterprise buying "an observability vendor" and an enterprise building observability as an in-house cross-cutting discipline are solving related but not identical problems, and conflating the two — assuming the vendor purchase discharges the cross-cutting need — is another concrete way the sold-as-available/run-as-unfinished gap from Chapter 6 shows up in practice.

## Chapter 8. Composability, Build vs. Buy, and Other Real Tradeoffs

None of the tradeoffs below has a universally correct answer, and both reports, read carefully, argue against treating any of them as if they did. FP shows a market where "vendors from markets as diverse as digital process automation (DPA), API management, search and retrieval, and customer service and support are all attempting to build AI platforms" [FP, Market Dynamics] — meaning the "one platform, one vendor" answer is being actively contested by the market itself, not just by architecture purists. FA shows an architecture discipline that explicitly rejects "a monolithic ideal state" [FA, Summary] in favor of modular, adaptable design chosen per use case.

**Managed platform vs. composable stack.**

| Dimension | Managed AI platform | Composable stack |
|---|---|---|
| Speed to first working system | Higher | Lower initially |
| Flexibility to swap a component | Lower | Higher |
| Operating burden | Lower — vendor absorbs ModelOps, some governance tooling | Higher — the org assembles and operates its own control plane |
| Vendor dependence | Higher | Lower |
| Governance integration | Pre-integrated, but only as mature as the vendor's own control-plane maturity — see Chapter 6 | Must be engineered explicitly, but the org controls its shape and can match it to FA's component-by-component maturity assessment |
| Fit with FP's functionality-by-use-case finding | Strong when the org's use case matches the vendor's core focus (Chapter 2's third capability) | Strong when the org's use cases span FP's extended use cases unevenly, since "AI platform solutions may less commonly address them" [FP, Top Use Cases] |

**One model vs. multi-model.** FA's runtime discussion is the relevant evidence here: because agent runtimes already have to tolerate degraded model behavior rather than binary uptime [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes], a multi-model architecture is not primarily a cost-optimization move — it's a reliability move, a way to route around a degraded provider the way the runtime already has to tolerate degraded *responses*. The cost is added orchestration complexity to route between models consistently.

**General-purpose platform vs. domain platform.** FP's functionality-by-use-case analysis shows that different use cases lean on genuinely different functionality clusters — customer intelligence needs data processing and predictive-model training; decision intelligence and automation needs agentic-flow tooling and fine-tuning/alignment precision; horizontal and industry applications need both assistant-style and agentic tooling with domain context [FP, Functionality By Use Case]. A platform strong on one cluster is not automatically strong on another, which argues against assuming "AI platform" functionality is fungible across use cases.

**Autonomy vs. control.** Already the subject of Chapter 6, worth restating as a tradeoff directly: more autonomy expands the action space an agent can take, which by the logic of Chapter 6 expands the failure surface, which raises — not lowers — the control-plane investment required to run it safely. Treating autonomy as a feature to maximize, independent of control-plane maturity, is the single tradeoff this article treats as least defensible, because it is the one both reports' evidence converges against most directly.

**Latency vs. reasoning depth**, and **quality vs. cost**, follow the same shape as the model-vs-system evaluation table in Chapter 7: a deeper reasoning loop, more tool calls, and more context assembly all raise latency and cost together, and FA's SLA-cost warning about execution-level guardrails applies here too — control isn't free, and its cost has to be budgeted alongside the reasoning it's controlling, not treated as a rounding error on top of it [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work].

## Chapter 9. Where Enterprises Get This Wrong

Each mistake below is tied to a specific claim from Chapters 1–8, not asserted independently.

**Mistake 1 — Treating model access as the entire platform.** Directly contradicted by Chapter 1: FP's own definition names model access as one input among several feeding "composable application components, AI agents, and/or complete applications" [FP, Market Definition]. Buying access to a strong model is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

**Mistake 2 — Buying an "agent platform" without understanding the execution architecture underneath it.** Chapter 5 showed that choosing single-agent, sequential, or orchestrator-based architecture changes memory shape, guardrail placement, access-control grain, and testability together. A vendor's "agent platform" label doesn't tell you which of these it implements, or which tradeoffs it made on your behalf.

**Mistake 3 — Letting agents call tools without granular authorization.** The tool-access-versus-tool-authorization distinction from Chapter 4, sharpened by Chapter 6's identity argument: tool *discoverability* is not the same question as whether *this* agent, acting for *this* user, in *this* context, should be allowed to invoke *this* tool right now.

**Mistake 4 — Adding autonomy before adding observability.** The direct consequence of Chapter 6's "larger action space, larger failure surface" argument, and of FA's own warning that "over-indexing on one layer alone... leaves critical failure modes exposed" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up].

**Mistake 5 — Evaluating model quality while ignoring end-to-end system quality.** Chapter 7's whole argument: every row of the model-centric-vs-system-centric table shows the same mismatch between what gets measured and what actually determines outcomes.

**Mistake 6 — Building dozens of agents where deterministic software would be better.** This is precisely what Forrester's Q4 2025 survey statistic describes: "agentic sprawl," where "teams can independently add agents, tools, prompts, and model endpoints, multiplying governance and monitoring overhead" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up] `[EMPIRICAL]`. Not every task that *can* be done by an agent *should* be — Chapter 5's sequential-multiagent pattern exists precisely because some workflows are linear enough that deterministic step boundaries outperform open-ended agent autonomy on testability and blast radius alone.

**Mistake 7 — Locking application logic too tightly to one model provider.** The multi-model reliability argument from Chapter 8, restated as a warning: if agent runtimes already have to tolerate degraded model behavior as a normal operating condition [FA, Strong Agentic Runtimes], an architecture with no way to route around one provider's degradation has no answer to a condition its own runtime is supposed to expect.

**Mistake 8 — Building retrieval without measuring retrieval quality.** FP names retrieval-adjacent use cases ("RAG and tool use" as an agentic-app component; RAG-focused vendors as active new entrants [FP, Figure 1; Market Maturity]) but retrieval quality doesn't appear as a metric on its own in either report — which is itself the point: if an organization's evaluation stays model-centric (Chapter 7), the retrieval step that feeds the model's context often goes unmeasured entirely, hidden inside "the prompt," even though bad retrieval produces the exact failure Chapter 7 names — "the model reasoned fine but... the context was wrong."

**Mistake 9 — Confusing demos with production architecture.** A single-agent demo, per Chapter 5, has none of the guardrail-per-step, access-control-per-task, or trajectory-observability properties an orchestrator-based production system gets almost for free by its shape. A demo that works is evidence the model is capable; it is not evidence the architecture is production-ready, because a demo usually skips the runtime distribution, degraded-mode handling, and step-level observability Chapter 4 and 5 describe as load-bearing, not optional.

**Mistake 10 — Ignoring lifecycle management.** Forrester's sprawl statistic again: sprawl "must be anticipated and managed through early operating discipline," with "baseline plans for cataloging agents, constraining tool surfaces, and enforcing evaluation and access controls before the long tail emerges" [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up]. This is explicitly a claim that lifecycle management has to be designed *before* the sprawl problem exists, not retrofitted after.

**Mistake 11 — Ignoring cost and latency amplification from multi-step execution.** Chapter 8's cost-of-control point, plus the direct interview warning that execution-level guardrails "are not free and should be factored into SLA costs" [FA, To Assign, Distribute, And Assemble Work]. A cost model built around a single inference call systematically underestimates what an orchestrated, multi-step, guardrail-checked trajectory actually costs.

**Mistake 12 — Assuming "human in the loop" automatically makes a system safe.** Neither report makes this claim directly, so it is flagged here as inference rather than citation `[SYNTHESIS]`: Chapter 4's guardrail discussion shows guardrails operate at multiple functional levels, from individual agent behavior to organizational policy — a single human checkpoint at one point in a trajectory does not substitute for guardrails distributed across the other levels, particularly in an orchestrator-based architecture where the orchestrator itself, not just the final output, makes consequential decisions (which agent runs next, which tool is permitted, whether to retry or escalate) that a single human review of the final answer never sees.

## Chapter 10. A Reference Architecture

`[SYNTHESIS]` **What follows is this article's own synthesis, informed by Forrester's platform diagram [FP, Figure 1] and eight-component breakdown [FA, The Eight Components Of Agentic Application Architecture], not a framework either report publishes as such. It is offered as one defensible way to organize the pieces both reports evidence, not as a claim about what any vendor sells or any analyst firm recommends.**

Read it top to bottom as the path a real request takes, and read the two side columns as the planes that constrain that path at every layer, not just at the edges:

- **Experience** — the interface a user or an upstream system actually touches: a copilot, an application, an API call.
- **Agent / workflow runtime** — Chapter 4's eight components live here: runtime, reasoning, memory, tool calling, guardrails, security, testing, orchestration, all colocated because none of them functions correctly in isolation from the others.
- **Orchestration** — Chapter 5's pattern choice: single agent, sequential, or orchestrator-based, each one reshaping the columns to its left and right, not just the row it sits in.
- **Models and context**, side by side rather than stacked — Chapter 1's claim made structural: neither box is "the system" on its own; context (retrieval, memory layers from Chapter 4) shapes what the model receives as much as the model's own weights shape what it returns.
- **Tools, APIs, and enterprise systems** — where reasoning becomes action, and where Chapter 4's authorization distinction has to be enforced, not merely declared.

Running the full height of the diagram, cross-cutting every layer rather than sitting at the bottom as an afterthought: **identity, policy, evaluation, and observability** — the control plane from Chapter 6, drawn as a plane precisely because Chapter 5 showed that a change at any one layer (adding an orchestrator, splitting memory, changing guardrail placement) changes what the control plane has to do, not just what the execution plane does.

The only claim this diagram makes that neither report makes explicitly is architectural: that the control plane belongs beside the stack, touching every layer, rather than underneath it as a foundation or above it as a final check. A foundation is built once and trusted afterward; Chapter 6's evidence — sprawl, immature IAM, execution-level guardrail cost — argues that this trust doesn't hold. The control plane has to be re-earned at every layer that changes, which is why it is drawn running alongside the stack rather than beneath it.

## Chapter 11. What This Means, Role by Role

**AI and ML engineers.** The evaluation shift in Chapter 7 is the most immediate operational change: a model-quality benchmark passing is not evidence the system is ready. Build trajectory-level test cases before scaling agent autonomy, not after (Mistake 4).

**Platform engineers.** Chapter 5's tradeoff table is a direct design brief: know, explicitly, which memory shape, guardrail placement, and access-control grain your chosen orchestration pattern produces, rather than discovering it in an incident review.

**Architects.** Chapter 3's lens distinction matters most here: a vendor scoring well on FP's functionality checklist has not discharged the architecture work FA describes as still largely unsolved, especially around identity. Treat a platform purchase as a starting inventory of components, not a finished control plane.

**Product managers.** Chapter 8's tradeoffs are product decisions disguised as infrastructure decisions — autonomy level, model diversity, and general-purpose versus domain-specific platform choice all trade directly against operating burden and control-plane investment, and none of them is free to reverse once users depend on the behavior.

**Technical leaders and CIO/CTO organizations.** Chapter 6 is the executive-level finding: budget the control plane as a first-class, ongoing engineering investment, not a governance line item bolted onto a platform purchase. Forrester's own sprawl statistic — 60% of decision-makers already naming it a challenge [FA, Agentic AI Architectures Emerge From The Top Down And Bottom Up] — is evidence this investment is currently under-made across the industry, not a hypothetical risk.

## What comes next

Neither report claims to have solved the control-plane problem this article describes; FA is explicit that the underlying practices are still immature, and FP is explicit that buyer confusion, not a settled standard, is the market's own top challenge. The structural implication is not that the model layer will keep improving and eventually make these problems moot — Chapter 1's evidence argues the opposite, that value and difficulty have already relocated away from the model. The open work, on the evidence in front of us, is in the plane running alongside the stack: identity built for delegated, autonomous actors; evaluation that watches trajectories instead of responses; observability that explains a decision instead of just recording that one was made. That is where the next round of real architectural disagreement — Gartner's included, when it becomes legitimately readable — will actually be fought.
